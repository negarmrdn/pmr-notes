# Stroke 1: Types, localization & acute management

!!! abstract "At a glance"
    About 87% of strokes are **ischemic**, about 10% are **intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH)** and about 3% are **subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH)**. For boards, know three things cold: the **stroke type** from the history, the **vascular territory** from the exam, and the **acute treatment rules** (thrombolysis, thrombectomy, blood pressure).

    Acute care follows the **2026 AHA/ASA guideline**, which replaced the 2018 guideline and its 2019 update. Boards may still use the older numbers, so both are noted where they differ.

    *Part 2 (aphasia, neglect, apraxia, dysphagia) and Part 3 (recovery, prognosis, complications) are coming next.*

## Epidemiology & risk factors

- About **795,000 strokes a year** in the US. Stroke is the **leading cause of long-term disability** and the **5th leading cause of death**.
- **#1 modifiable risk factor: hypertension.**
- **Atrial fibrillation:** about 5× the risk. It is the most common cardiac source of emboli.
- **Age:** risk **doubles each decade after 55**.
- **Race and sex:** Black Americans have about 2× the risk. Men have a higher age-specific incidence, but more women die of stroke overall because they live longer.
- **Other:** diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia, prior stroke or TIA, carotid disease, heavy alcohol use, cocaine.

### TIA

- The current definition is **tissue-based**: transient focal brain, spinal cord or retinal ischemia with **no infarct on imaging**. The old "under 24 hours" rule is outdated.
- Stroke risk after a TIA is highest in the **first 48 hours**.

**ABCD2 score** (0–7; **≥4 = high risk**):

| Item | Points |
|---|---|
| **A**ge ≥60 | 1 |
| **B**P ≥140/90 | 1 |
| **C**linical: unilateral weakness | 2 |
| **C**linical: speech problem without weakness | 1 |
| **D**uration ≥60 min | 2 |
| **D**uration 10–59 min | 1 |
| **D**iabetes | 1 |

## Stroke types

TOAST classification of ischemic stroke, plus the two hemorrhagic types.

| Type | Mechanism | Clinical clues | Board pearls |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Large-artery atherosclerosis (thrombotic)** | Plaque plus clot in situ, or artery-to-artery embolism | **Stepwise or stuttering** course, often noticed **on waking**, often preceded by TIAs | Most common site: **carotid bifurcation / ICA origin**. Amaurosis fugax = ophthalmic artery (first ICA branch) |
| **Cardioembolic** | AF, LV thrombus, valve disease, endocarditis, paradoxical embolus through a PFO | **Deficit is maximal at onset**, often during activity, can hit several territories | **MCA is the most common territory.** Hemorrhagic transformation is more common |
| **Small vessel (lacunar)** | **Lipohyalinosis** of penetrating arteries from chronic HTN (also DM) | Infarct under 15 mm with **no cortical signs** (no aphasia, neglect or field cut) | **Best prognosis.** Lenticulostriate, thalamoperforating and pontine paramedian arteries |
| **Other determined cause** | Dissection, hypercoagulable state, vasculitis, sickle cell, moyamoya, CADASIL | Young patient. Dissection: **neck pain plus Horner syndrome** | Think of these in patients under 45 |
| **Cryptogenic** | No cause found after a full workup | | Raises the PFO question (see secondary prevention) |
| **ICH** | Vessel rupture into the brain tissue | Headache, vomiting, falling level of consciousness, **worsens over minutes to hours** | **HTN is the #1 cause** |
| **SAH** | Usually a ruptured saccular (berry) aneurysm | **Thunderclap** headache ("worst of my life"), meningismus | Trauma is the most common cause overall. **Aneurysm is the most common non-traumatic cause** |

### Lacunar syndromes

| Syndrome | Location |
|---|---|
| **Pure motor** (most common) | Posterior limb of the internal capsule, or basis pontis |
| **Pure sensory** | **VPL nucleus of the thalamus** |
| **Ataxic hemiparesis** | Basis pontis, or internal capsule |
| **Dysarthria–clumsy hand** | Basis pontis, or genu of the internal capsule |
| **Sensorimotor** | Thalamus plus internal capsule |

## Localization: anterior circulation

| Artery | Key findings |
|---|---|
| **ACA** | Contralateral **leg > arm** weakness and sensory loss, face spared. Abulia, **urinary incontinence**, grasp and suck reflexes, gait apraxia. Dominant side: **transcortical motor aphasia**. Corpus callosum: left-hand apraxia, alien hand |
| **MCA (stem)** | Contralateral **face and arm > leg** weakness and sensory loss, homonymous hemianopia, **eyes deviate toward the lesion** |
| **MCA superior division** | Face and arm weakness. Dominant side: **Broca aphasia** (non-fluent) |
| **MCA inferior division** | Often little weakness. Dominant side: **Wernicke aphasia**. Superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky", Meyer's loop) |
| **Dominant parietal** | **Gerstmann syndrome** (angular gyrus): agraphia, acalculia, finger agnosia, right–left confusion. Ideomotor apraxia |
| **Non-dominant parietal** | **Neglect**, anosognosia, constructional and dressing apraxia, aprosody, impulsivity (poor safety awareness, so often a longer rehab stay) |
| **PCA** | Contralateral hemianopia **with macular sparing** (MCA collaterals supply the occipital pole). Memory loss (hippocampus). **Dejerine–Roussy** thalamic pain syndrome (sensory loss, then burning pain) |
| **Dominant PCA plus splenium** | **Alexia without agraphia**: can write but can't read what they wrote |
| **Bilateral PCA** | Cortical blindness. **Anton syndrome** (blind but denies it). Prosopagnosia. **Balint syndrome** (bilateral parieto-occipital: optic ataxia, oculomotor apraxia, simultanagnosia) |
| **Anterior choroidal** | Triad: **hemiplegia, hemisensory loss, hemianopia** |
| **Watershed (ACA–MCA border zone)** | From hypotension or low flow. Proximal arm and hip weakness ("**man in a barrel**" when bilateral). Transcortical aphasias |

### Gaze rules

| Lesion | Eyes deviate |
|---|---|
| Cortical (frontal eye field) | **Toward the lesion**, away from the weak side |
| Pons | **Away from the lesion**, toward the weak side |
| Seizure focus (irritative) | **Away from the focus** |
| Thalamic hemorrhage | **Downward** ("peering at the nose") |

## Localization: posterior circulation & brainstem

**Crossed findings** (cranial nerve deficit on the same side as the lesion, body deficit on the opposite side) mean **brainstem**. Other posterior circulation clues: diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, dizziness, drop attacks, ataxia, nystagmus.

| Syndrome | Location | Same side as lesion | Opposite side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Weber** | Ventral midbrain (PCA penetrating branches) | **CN III palsy** | **Hemiplegia** (cerebral peduncle) |
| **Benedikt** | Midbrain tegmentum (red nucleus) | CN III palsy | **Tremor, ataxia, involuntary movements**, ± hemiparesis |
| **Parinaud** | Dorsal midbrain (classically a pineal tumor) | Upgaze palsy, convergence–retraction nystagmus, light–near dissociation | |
| **Millard–Gubler** | Ventral pons | **CN VI and CN VII palsy** | Hemiplegia |
| **Foville** | Dorsal pons | CN VII palsy, **horizontal gaze palsy toward the lesion** | Hemiplegia |
| **Locked-in** | **Both sides of the ventral pons (basilar artery)** | | Quadriplegia and anarthria, with **consciousness, vertical gaze and blinking preserved** |
| **Lateral medullary (Wallenberg)** | **Vertebral artery (most often) or PICA** | **Loss of pain and temperature on the face**, **Horner syndrome**, ataxia, **dysphagia and hoarseness** (nucleus ambiguus), vertigo, nystagmus, hiccups | **Loss of pain and temperature on the body** |
| **Medial medullary (Dejerine)** | Anterior spinal artery or vertebral artery | **Tongue deviates toward the lesion** (CN XII) | Hemiparesis, loss of proprioception and vibration |
| **Top of the basilar** | Distal basilar artery | Visual, eye movement and behavioral changes, sleepiness, often little weakness | |

!!! note "Rehab angle"
    - **Wallenberg has no limb weakness.** The main rehab problem is **dysphagia**, which often needs a temporary PEG. Overall prognosis is good.
    - **Cerebellar infarct or bleed:** vertigo, vomiting, inability to walk, ataxia on the side of the lesion. Swelling can cause **hydrocephalus or brainstem compression**, which may need suboccipital decompression.

## Hemorrhagic stroke

### Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH)

**Locations, most to least common:** putamen > lobar ≈ thalamus > cerebellum ≈ pons.

| Location | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Putamen** | Contralateral hemiparesis, eyes deviate toward the lesion |
| **Thalamus** | Sensory loss, eyes deviate downward |
| **Pons** | Coma, **pinpoint pupils**, quadriparesis. Worst prognosis |
| **Cerebellum** | Vertigo, vomiting, ataxia, can't walk. No hemiparesis |
| **Lobar, in an elderly patient** | Think **cerebral amyloid angiopathy**. Tends to recur. Microbleeds on GRE/SWI MRI |

- **Prognosis:** bleed volume (estimate with **ABC/2**) and GCS are the main predictors. The **ICH score** uses GCS, volume ≥30 mL, bleeding into the ventricles, location below the tentorium, and age ≥80.
- **Board pearl:** early mortality is higher than for ischemic stroke, but survivors **can recover more than ischemic patients with similar early deficits**, because the clot resorbs and the pressure comes off.

**Management:**

- Lower SBP to about **140**, and avoid going below 130 (2022 AHA ICH guideline).
- Reverse anticoagulation:
    - **Warfarin:** 4-factor PCC plus IV vitamin K
    - **Dabigatran:** idarucizumab
    - **Factor Xa inhibitors:** 4-factor PCC or a specific reversal agent, per hospital protocol
- **No preventive antiseizure drugs.**
- **Cerebellar ICH over 3 cm, or with brainstem compression or hydrocephalus, needs surgical evacuation.**

### Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH)

- **Aneurysm sites:** **ACom** is most common, then PCom and MCA.
- **Diagnosis:** non-contrast CT is most sensitive in the first 6 hours. If the CT is negative and suspicion is high, do an LP and look for **xanthochromia**.
- **Grading:** Hunt–Hess (clinical) and Fisher (amount of blood on CT; predicts vasospasm).

| Complication | Timing and treatment |
|---|---|
| **Rebleeding** | Highest risk in the **first 24 hours**, so the aneurysm is secured early (coiling or clipping) |
| **Vasospasm** | **Days 3–14**, peak around days 7–10. Causes delayed ischemic deficits |
| **Hydrocephalus** | External ventricular drain (EVD) |
| **Low sodium** | **Cerebral salt wasting**: volume depleted, give saline, **don't restrict fluids**. **SIADH**: normal volume, restrict fluids |
| **Seizures** | |

- **Nimodipine 60 mg every 4 hours for 21 days** improves outcome **but does not prevent vasospasm** on angiography.
- "Triple-H therapy" is outdated. Current practice is normal volume status, with induced hypertension if vasospasm causes symptoms.

## Acute ischemic stroke: step by step

1. **History and exam.** Establish the **last known well** time, screen with BE-FAST, and score the **NIHSS**.
2. **Fingerstick glucose.** Hypoglycemia is the classic stroke mimic. Glucose is the only lab needed before a thrombolytic unless you suspect a bleeding disorder.
3. **Non-contrast CT head** to **rule out hemorrhage**. CT is often normal early in ischemia; look for a hyperdense MCA sign. **MRI DWI is the most sensitive test** for acute ischemia and turns positive within minutes.
4. **CTA head and neck** to look for a large vessel occlusion (LVO). Don't wait for a creatinine before giving contrast.
5. **IV thrombolysis** if eligible (below).
6. **Thrombectomy** if there's an LVO (below).
7. **Blood pressure, glucose, antiplatelets and supportive care** (below).

### IV thrombolysis

| Drug | Dose |
|---|---|
| **Tenecteplase** (FDA-approved for stroke in March 2025) | **0.25 mg/kg**, max 25 mg, single IV bolus |
| **Alteplase** | **0.9 mg/kg**, max 90 mg. 10% as a bolus over 1 minute, the rest over 60 minutes |

- **Window: within 4.5 hours** of onset or last known well. Tenecteplase and alteplase are **equally preferred** in the 2026 guideline.
- **Not for mild, non-disabling symptoms**, meaning symptoms that don't affect ADLs or the ability to return to work.
- **Extended window (2026), with salvageable tissue on automated perfusion imaging:** symptoms on waking within 9 hours of the midpoint of sleep, or 4.5–9 hours from last known well.
- **Unknown onset beyond 4.5 hours:** an MRI **DWI–FLAIR mismatch** can help select patients.

!!! danger "Classic contraindications"
    - Any hemorrhage on CT
    - BP over 185/110 that can't be lowered
    - Ischemic stroke, serious head trauma or intracranial surgery within **3 months**
    - GI bleed within **21 days**
    - Platelets under 100,000, INR over 1.7, a DOAC within 48 hours, or full-dose LMWH within 24 hours
    - Brain tumor (intra-axial), infective endocarditis, aortic dissection

### Endovascular thrombectomy (EVT)

| Situation | Criteria (2026) |
|---|---|
| **ICA or M1 occlusion, within 6 h** | NIHSS ≥6, pre-stroke mRS 0–1, ASPECTS 3–10 |
| **6–24 h** | Selected patients based on imaging. Some patients with large infarct cores now qualify, which is the big expansion |
| **Basilar occlusion, within 24 h** | Baseline mRS 0–1, NIHSS ≥10, PC-ASPECTS ≥6 |

### Blood pressure

| Situation | Target |
|---|---|
| **Before a thrombolytic** | Under **185/110** |
| **After a thrombolytic** | Under **180/105** for 24 hours. **No antithrombotics for 24 hours** |
| **No reperfusion therapy** | Permissive hypertension up to **220/120** for the first 24–48 hours |

!!! warning "Don't push BP too low (2026)"
    - After IV thrombolysis for mild-to-moderate stroke, an SBP target under 140 (instead of under 180) gives **no benefit**.
    - After successful thrombectomy, an SBP target under 140 for the first 72 hours is **harmful**.

### Other early management

- **Glucose:** avoid both low and high glucose; the usual target is about 140–180. Driving glucose down to 80–130 with IV insulin does **not** improve 3-month outcomes.
- **Aspirin** within 24–48 hours. Wait 24 hours if a thrombolytic was given.
- **Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT):** for minor non-cardioembolic stroke (**NIHSS ≤5**) or high-risk TIA (**ABCD2 ≥4**), give clopidogrel plus aspirin for **21 days**, then one antiplatelet. Older sources say NIHSS ≤3, based on the POINT and CHANCE trials.
- **AF anticoagulation timing:** starting early is reasonable in selected patients, usually those with milder strokes. Older board questions use the "**1-3-6-12**" rule: start on day 1 after a TIA, day 3 after a mild stroke, day 6 after a moderate stroke and day 12 after a severe stroke.
- **Malignant MCA swelling:** decompressive hemicraniectomy **within 48 hours** (strongest evidence at age ≤60). It lowers mortality, but many survivors are left with significant disability.

!!! note "PM&R angle in the acute phase"
    - **Dysphagia screen before anything by mouth.**
    - **DVT prevention:** intermittent pneumatic compression, plus LMWH or heparin if the patient isn't moving.
    - **AVERT trial:** very early, high-dose mobilization **within 24 hours led to worse 3-month outcomes**. Early mobilization is good; aggressive mobilization in the first 24 hours is not.

## Secondary prevention

- **Carotid stenosis with symptoms (NASCET):**
    - **70–99%:** carotid endarterectomy (CEA), ideally **within 2 weeks**
    - **50–69%:** consider it; men benefit more
    - **Under 50%:** medical treatment only
    - Stenting is an alternative. Older patients do better with CEA.
- **Statin:** high intensity, LDL goal under 70.
- **Blood pressure:** under 130/80 long term.
- **AF:** a DOAC rather than warfarin, **except** with a mechanical valve or moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis (use warfarin).
- **PFO closure:** age 18–60 with a non-lacunar cryptogenic stroke and high-risk PFO features.
- Smoking cessation, diabetes control, exercise.

## High-yield for boards

!!! tip "Board pearls"
    - **Most common:** cause of ICH = **HTN**. Source of emboli = **AF**. Site of atherosclerosis = **carotid bifurcation**. Lacunar syndrome = **pure motor**. ICH location = **putamen**. Aneurysm site = **ACom**.
    - **Lacunar strokes have no cortical signs.** Aphasia, neglect or a field cut means the lesion is cortical.
    - **Leg > arm = ACA. Face and arm > leg = MCA.**
    - **Locked-in:** awake, with vertical eye movements and blinking preserved (basilar artery, ventral pons).
    - **SAH vasospasm: days 3–14.** Nimodipine improves outcome.
    - **AVERT:** no very early, high-dose mobilization in the first 24 hours.

!!! warning "Easy to mix up"
    - **Macular sparing = PCA.** No macular sparing, plus weakness = MCA.
    - **Lateral medullary:** pain and temperature loss on the **face on the lesion side and the body on the other side**, with **no weakness**. **Medial medullary:** weakness, and the tongue deviates **toward** the lesion.
    - **Thrombotic:** stepwise, often on waking. **Embolic:** maximal at onset, during activity.
    - **Nimodipine** improves outcome but **does not prevent** vasospasm.
    - **Cerebral salt wasting: give fluid. SIADH: restrict fluid.**
    - **Cortical lesion:** eyes look toward the lesion. **Pontine lesion:** eyes look away from it.

## Quick self-quiz

??? question "Left CN III palsy plus right hemiplegia. Name the syndrome and the location."
    **Weber syndrome**, left ventral midbrain (cerebral peduncle), from PCA penetrating branches.

??? question "Hoarseness, trouble swallowing, left Horner syndrome, loss of pain sensation on the left face and right body, no weakness. Which artery?"
    **Left lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome**, most often from the vertebral artery (or PICA).

??? question "Can write a sentence but can't read it back. Where is the lesion?"
    **Dominant (left) PCA territory plus the splenium of the corpus callosum** (alexia without agraphia).

??? question "Right leg weakness much greater than the arm, urinary incontinence, abulia. Which artery?"
    **Left ACA.**

??? question "Day 6 after aneurysmal SAH, the patient becomes confused with new right arm weakness. What's happening?"
    **Vasospasm** (days 3–14), which causes delayed cerebral ischemia.

??? question "Before a thrombolytic, BP is 200/115. What's the target?"
    Lower it to **under 185/110** before giving the drug, then keep it **under 180/105** for 24 hours afterward.
